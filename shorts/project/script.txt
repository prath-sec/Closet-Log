Roman buildings are still standing because the Romans mixed their concrete badly.
Rome, around the year one hundred and twenty. Builders are pouring the biggest dome on Earth.
They've got volcanic ash, broken rock, water, and one very hot ingredient: quicklime.
Drop quicklime into a wet mix and it boils. The whole pile steams like a soup pot.
When it cools, the concrete is full of little white lumps. Chalky crumbs, everywhere.
For centuries, experts looked at those lumps and sighed. Sloppy work. Lazy stirring. Typical Romans.
Meanwhile, the Romans kept building. Harbors, aqueducts, bridges, and a giant dome called the Pantheon.

When a crack creeps through the concrete, it runs straight into one of those lumps.
Rain seeps in. The water dissolves the calcium and carries it into the crack.
Then it hardens into fresh stone and seals the gap. Like a scab on a scraped knee.
The concrete heals itself. Every lump is a tiny repair kit, waiting for the rain.
And the boiling hot mix is what made those lumps crumbly enough to work.
In the sea, it gets even stranger. Roman harbor walls grow new crystals in salt water.
